## Sara

> “Imagine opening a journal that only published the most unserious academic takes”

> “BRO” 

It’s a short exchange, but one that ended up planting the seeds for the website you see today. Born out of a sentiment shared by my fellow Nerds, Farheen and Benicio, Nerds *Unbridled* is meant to create a repository for thoughts which may not have a home anywhere else. 

Nerds as we may be, we have a troubled relationship with formatting and citing as much as you do. Let me be clear that I’m speaking for myself here, but I think that academic formats are often a necessary evil: they establish the “rules of the game” of academic knowledge production, which end up giving meaning to the practice itself. 
I have been developing my  thoughts on this for a while now because it was on my mind a lot  when I was a competitive debater. To do decently well in a debate tournament, you had to memorise a certain set of rules, respect them all to avoid point deductions and some teams would go as far as signalling rule violations to the judges by knocking loudly on their desks if you slipped up. What would often cross my mind when I first started out was: ‘Why were the rules so important - especially since we are debating, a context designed for ideas to be shared?’ Surely it’s more important that my idea is good rather than if I’m abiding by rules as arbitrary as “speak for precisely eight minutes”. 

But over time, my frustration became appreciation for what the rules were doing. The rules actually forced you to tackle substance. You needed to not introduce new material later in the debate because you had to deal with what was already said; so when moving sideways was forbidden, you could only go down. The more in-depth you argued, the more points you were awarded. Rules like “No new material in third or whip speeches” or “No asking questions in the first and final minutes of a speech” force debaters away from petty tactics  which focus on making your opponent look bad and away from superficial analysis which leans on populist tropes. The rules were like a centre of gravity, which pulled debates into intellectually honest, substance-based and analysis driven discourse. 

Academic writing rules, in my mind, are doing something similar. There’s plenty of arguments to be made about how effective they are in achieving their goal, but the underlying idea that they are there to preserve accountability and scientific method is one that I think is needed for good research. That being said: although the rules exist for some very valid reasons, we don’t always need to be academics to have and share interesting, nuanced thoughts.

So in sum: credit where credit is due. However, ideas flourish in many different environments and in following different rules. Each format prioritises different things, so only producing and consuming knowledge through academia would limit our understanding, creativity and curiosity, by forcing it through a singular medium. Arguably, only consulting academic writing would actually run counter to a holistic pursuit of knowledge. 

Nothing replaces late night debates, sometimes encouraged by a few beers, in between semesters and unburdened by deadlines, taken *deeply* seriously for their duration, just for the sake of it. This is what we’re calling *“Unbridled”*! Someday we’ll recount here the hour-long conversation we had on whether dogs are all fascists (followed by an equally long conversation about which animals are inherently anti-fascist - which btw, now that I type it out, I realise is a pretty bioessentialist take).

If some of you reading are still skeptical and think I lack the sufficient gravitas for these lofty takes of mine, let me refer you to the Ancients. Their long beards and the air of mysticism conferred by the centuries and centuries which separate them from you and me might paint a more credible, erudite picture and be more appealing to some of you. 

Socrates and his deferent students, Plato among them, would spend hours walking under the porticoed walkways of the schools they taught in, surrounded by their students.

They didn’t do this because they were eccentrics, but because they thought it was a necessary practice for effective learning. It was a pedagogical method known under the name “peripathos”, literally meaning “walking around”. It’s now also calling to mind the Australian aboriginal tradition of “walk about”, when a boy would leave his community for a period of up to six months, prove that he had mastered the teachings he grew up with and come back an adult. The walkabout marked a boy’s coming of age and was an essential cornerstone for absorbing the teachings he had received from his community. 

Drawing a connection from Greece and across to Australia, I wonder if there may be some kind of ancestral urge to seek knowledge from experience, outside of libraries, outside of the formatted written word. We learn something different when we engage with the world instead of just studying it. 

Now clearly, our project is not in the same realm as the Walkabout nor of the peripathos: we’re not actually entering a physical space and are arguably still just writing thoughts while sitting behind a screen. Not much “experiencing the world” happening here, much like regular bookish study. However, I would still argue that we are paying a small tribute to the idea that there is no one way of learning or of producing knowledge. By setting aside rigid academic formats, following the path set out by an idea that crosses your mind and sharing it with whomever wants to read it, is starting a conversation *ex novo* - which is instigating human contact and interaction. 


A big hope that all us editors (that’s cool to say!) share is that Nerds *Unbridled* will be about building a community. We have lots of ideas that will come to fruition in the near future, and it’s fun to watch our bubbling enthusiasm come together and spread to the friends with whom we shared this idea of ours. 

But I’ll leave the rest to my fellow Nerds. Why were you guys so excited for Nerds *Unbridled*?

## Farheen 

I have never enjoyed academic writing. 

I do not share Sara’s enthusiasm about the necessity of the rules of academic writing. I perceive there to be a not-so-subtly hidden disdain for creativity; for the flowery language of emotional appeal that objectivity shakes it head at in disappointment; for the distraction of tangential points and anecdotes bearing little gravity for its evidence-based conclusions; and for the long run-on sentences that only know commas and semi-colons, like the breathless, incessant child discovering the concept of questions for the first time. 

But my disagreement should not be read as condemnation of academic writing as a regime, nor am I arguing that there is no place for it. We are, simply put, not compatible. My pen is happiest without constraints, when the only rule that matters is that I need to pursue one idea at a time, even if not all of them fill a gap in existing literature (even when creative writing often does purport to fill gaps, of perspectives and stories untold) or make a poignant point or observation about the world around me; something exists simply because I thought of it. 

All admonishes towards this confession that are currently forming in your minds are not without purchase. I voluntarily chose a research degree for my Master’s, spent three years prior to it as a researcher, and the three years before that pursuing another research degree. It started out as a belief, naive perhaps, that my love of writing and my anger toward injustice could have a fruitful marriage in social sciences. I suppose I found a balance there in all these years, but it has never risen to *enjoyment*. And the more I do it – there is so much to be angry about – the less I find myself wanting to. Each keyboard click is heavier than the last. Unsurprisingly, along the way, the freedom I regarded so precious to my writing did not seem to matter anymore, and my pen remained *clicked* - closed. 

But where the writing stopped, the long sequence from thought to output only became stronger. So, Sara asked, why am I excited about Nerds *Unbridled*? Because here is a medium that unburdens the formal process of academic writing, and relaxes the cautiousness of academic thinking. 

Where would we be if sofa conversations over a cup of tea did not exist? What would we know about ourselves as individuals and as a society if all observations worth engaging with were only peer reviews? We’ve spent countless hours finding the profound in the ordinary conversations around our small dinner tables here in Geneva. The glorious advantage of being immersed in an academic setting with people ready to engage with diatribes and inane discussions alike has ignited an enthusiasm to keep pushing the circle outward. That’s what we hope this project will be. 

I imagine it as a process of thinking out loud. In an age where media literacy is being relegated to urban legends and all sorts of softwares are being developed to do the thinking for us, it’s an act of rebellion (as cheesy as it sounds) not to give in, and to continue the process of thinking. End results, in my opinion, are not important here. The process is what makes it real; putting it out there to be engaged with, developed, improved, fed, and nurtured; we’ve all done roundtables and conferences and informal chats and requests for feedback, it’s a natural desire to engage. That’s the other hope we have for this project, to build a sense of community, for the serious, the unserious, and everything in between. 

There’s an argument to be made against the need to document the spontaneity of conversations, which is perhaps not even possible to translate as accurately to paper. But then I think of the feeling I experience when the pieces of an argument just fall into place as I talk, or a conversation unsettles the mud on an idea I had not realised was buried deep in my mind somewhere, or the adrenaline in bouncing off the energy my friends bring to the conversation - and how I often let the momentum fail. Documentation is my idea of persevering, and so Nerds *Unbridled* is to be a record of our time and our mind, long after times have changed and minds have been lost. 

Alas, admittedly, Nerds *Unbridled* is not as free from the rules of academic writing as I would have liked it to be. My fellow nerds counter my anarchy by making an (entirely valid) point about the importance of some form of structure that guides the publication. It seems we are not as ready to leave behind our collective academic backgrounds as I thought. Just as well; I confess I have a feeling of relief knowing there is something familiar for our future nerds to contend with. But it has that freedom of the mind that lets me draw parallels between dictators and celebrities, present it as seriously as I would an essay on the drawbacks of paper ballots, and that is enough for me.

## Benicio

So here it is. Beyond the test articles about the Pringles man and lord knows whatever else made its way onto our website during the testing phase of this operation, Nerds *Unbridled* now exists. 

The question at stake now is that of purpose. What is the purpose of Nerds *Unbridled*? And how do we envision it being unique? I’d like to begin by framing my longer response to this question in two shorter scenes.

**The first scene** drawn here is of a student, a lonely burgeoning academic with her candlelit desk, chamomile teapot perfuming her dorm, and Google Docs open. There's a paper due in a week—she decided not to procrastinate—so she takes her time. She writes about history, politics, love, the future, and all of her once-subliminal thoughts begin to meander on the page in front of her.

After a week, she's done. The paper is submitted, the professor gives an astounding mark, and for a brief period she feels that the whole week behind her genuinely was an enjoyable time—despite what she might occasionally tell her friends—and that paper may have even been genuinely fun to write.

And then it’s gone.

Weeks and months pass, new deadlines arise and the joy, the product, those meandering thoughts, are forever imprisoned in cloud storage.

**The second scene** is probably also a familiar one. A few students this time, not lonely and romantic, but animated by good spirits (I will let you decide what "spirits" mean in this context). Their candlelit desk is a sunny day and a grass field overlooking a lake they can’t see the end of. Someone in the group poses a question: "Do you think Foucault would like ChatGPT?" 

Someone else takes a gamble: “Yeah, he definitely would.” 

A disagreement arises: "To be honest, I feel like he would see it as a bit of a digital panopticon.” 

The gambler: “Maybe, but I reckon he'd see it as a means to the full realization of the individual.” 

The disagreer: “How on earth can ChatGPT realize the individual??”

And so it goes, for hours on end. Some in the group called themselves Foucaultian. Others have never read a word of Foucault, and they all debate just the same. But eventually the topic of conversation changes. Someone has to run to catch the bus home, others go to get groceries before the store closes, and as the group dissipates, so too does the conversation and that peculiar Foucaultian-AI dialectic.

In some sense, our goal here at Nerds *Unbridled*, is to change the fate of the disappointed high-spirited academic and those lakeside socratics. What if that paper didn’t have to just live in the clouds, and that conversation had a place where others could listen in and record it? That is what this is. Farheen called it a place to think out loud, a place to document in an effort to preserve. I envision Nerds *Unbridled* as an effort to document a living dialogue, so Sara’s peripathos can be witnessed by all, and anyone can join in.

As a serial student myself, I recognize how often it is that our work goes to waste, and thoughtlings that might have created something of value are readily disregarded. "Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them,” said productivity author David Allen. Forgetting is easier than creating, and so too is it easy to think that I’m not capable of coming up with great thoughts. But it always starts small. A word, sentence, drawing, or conversation. I want to encourage everyone to place ideas—no matter how seemingly unimportant—onto a sheet of (maybe digital) paper. By writing it down, you've put the little thought-fish that was flopping on land into a little bowl of water. But the fish still needs more; it needs the sea.

That's when you need to take that work and put it online. Unafraid of the rigorous and pretentious standards imposed by the gatekeepers of intellect. I firmly believe we are capable of letting the world read our half-baked thoughts. Indeed, that is when they might be at their most valuable.

Is there value in external restraint? In answering this question, I admittedly find myself teetering between Sara and Farheen.  But I should like to qualify my answer a bit more and identify where exactly restraint should exist for the *best* writing. It is not the case that having questions to answer and rigid guidelines produces good writing; good writing comes from *editing*, not *producing*. I believe that writers should be left to their own devices in producing a first draft of their work. I often describe my writing process as me first emptying my fridge, and then picking the ingredients for my final dish. But I don’t just toss whole carrots into the pot; peeling, chopping, discarding the rotten and rooty bits, and seasoning is all necessary. Sorry, I’m hungry.

I believe writers should be encouraged to produce words and feel unconstrained when doing so. In this, I sympathize with Farheen’s argument: we must document those ideas which we spontaneously think of. Being held back from laying down their words is one of the most demotivating obstacles a writer can feel. So with Nerds *Unbridled*, I want people to grant themselves permission to throw those spontaneous thoughts onto a page. The purpose of our guidelines, our themes, and our “conversation” feature is to give people a starting point. You may choose to follow our themes or participate in a conversation, but they are not strictly necessary. Belaboring my hunger analogy: we provide some recipe ideas, and it is up to you to decide which you should like to follow.

But don’t stop there. I submit that this logic should guide your first draft ethos. But no first draft is error-free. For one to be a good writer, editing must take center stage. That is when restraints are most useful. Having guidelines, themes, and questions to answer should not prevent you from putting all of your thoughts onto a page, but if you want to be a *good* writer, you must identify and crop those wonderful ideas which do not contribute to the point you are trying to make. It is indeed the case that a passenger will prefer to not take a long-distance train with too many stops. Provide your readers with an express option, and they will ride it much farther. 

So feel free to live here for a bit. Have a read around—there might not be much for a while—but we're working on it. Reach out to us with any of your shower thoughts. I’d like to make the final note  that although I often find myself falling into a cycle of pretentiousness, I hope this becomes a space where people are incentivized to write without (at least some) of the shackles of high-academia. A place for ideas to grow together.

I would also like to thank Sara and Farheen for working with me to bring this project to life. Here is a task for you, dear reader: Dig through your file cabinet (digital or not), and see what's been marinating in there. You might come across a mini polemic you wrote for an old English class, or some assorted late-night thoughts in an old journal. Rewrite it. Make it structured and argumentative. Then send it our way. Who knows, it may be on a front cover soon.

Or, if you so dare, contribute to this roundtable by commenting below!

Until next time, 

The Nerd Editors
