_**Note:** The following is a cowritten paper for a University course on Counterinsurgency and Counterterrorism._


<hr>
<br>

Women's marginalized position in society places them in precarious situations across  contexts, positioned by states, capitalism, counter insurgents, and insurgents. Women as a whole  are frequently utilized as a rhetorical talking point to serve agendas. Women are under a triple bind,  as the founder of the PKK, Ocalan describes. Under patriarchy, women's submission to the male class is extracted through physical and social punishment. Their role is clear: to produce future  agents of capital, perpetuate the nuclear family as an economic unit, to support male breadwinners.  Under the state, which acts as a father figure, both paternalistic and threatening while offering  “protection” (protection from whom? Other men), women are supposed to sacrifice their bodies  for the state, also in the form of sexual reproduction: to produce soldiers and patriots. Capitalism is inherent to these state and patriarchal logics. The three themes identified here are Securofeminism,  the need for a reconceptualized feminism in the form of jineologî, and how Class and Capital  interact with Empire and Feminism. The last subject will be the one to which this paper provides  the largest contribution.  

States have mobilized two agendas to instrumentalize women in their capital and  patriarchy-driven imperial and carceral aims: Women, Peace, and Security (WPS) and Countering  Violent Extremism (CVE). As an ideological starting point, these two agendas, working in tandem,  should understand the impact of conflict on women in particular, but when co-opted by states,  they also take on an implicit Islamophobic, elitist, and nation-state security discourse, based  inherently on a plethora of contradictions. One such contradiction is that between the conflicting  needs of the nation-state and capital in their temporal and spatial needs; the nation-state being  based on strict territoriality, and capital being based on continuous human and resource  exploitation. These contradictions will be seen to play an important role in the exclusion of women  based in the “periphery” as understood in World-System Theory. The two agendas benefit largely  from a weaponization of a certain feminist standpoint epistemology—which one may call  bioessentialist, to be discussed shortly—which posits the necessity for women to be included in the  actions of the state against “extremism” (i.e., that which threatens state hegemony). This is what is  meant by Securofeminism (Abu-Lughod, 2023, p. 89). One may observe various modern state  administrations, which, by pure quantitative metrics, are more representative of women, like the  current Trump administration, but, because of their inherent ignorance of intersectional needs,  serve (perhaps on an even stronger epistemic basis) the interests of the capital-owning class. So,  from the hegemonic core, feminism, class, and ideology serve a mutually reinforcing role, with  positivist non-intersectional feminism creating a stronger epistemic framework on which a  hegemonic nation-state can exert its power on an “Othered” group, which could not exist if not for an exclusionary form of feminism. Additionally, Securofeminism instrumentalizes women in  counterinsurgency movements: the state’s use the rhetorical tool of 'women's rights' to justify the  invasion and sustained occupation in certain countries, as seen with the US invasion and  occupation of Afghanistan in 2001. Notably, discussions of women's rights and freedoms go out  the door when the question of bombing cancer and maternity hospitals in Palestine arises.  Securofeminism legitimizes state violence “on behalf” of women, ultimately dehumanizing them, recognizing their agency insofar as it relates to their positions as mothers, (moral saints) as seen in  the example Abu-Lughod presents on MotherSchools, programs designed to train mothers to  deradicalize their sons.  

From the periphery, women and feminism are mobilized in ways that both further  State-Capital-Feminism contradictions. Shamima Begum was a British national who was stripped  of her British citizenship after she left the UK when she was 15 to go to Syria and join the Islamic State (IS). The decision was upheld by British courts on appeal. The reasoning was simple from the  state: Begum presented a national security threat; if allowed back in the UK, she would be a  pertinent threat to British life, because her “extremist” dispositions may lead her to commit acts of  terror in the UK. The Telegraph, in their reporting on Begum in Syria, affirmed state suspicions  that she served a very active role in the IS, particularly as a “moral enforcer” of the group’s  ideological code, which would include strict repression against her women compatriots. The  allegations were denied by her and those closest to her in the IS. Regardless of the veracity of the  claims, entities such as the UN and various states promulgated a “jihadist bride” stereotype based  on them, describing how traditional feminine outlooks would lead those involved in CVE to  underestimate the role of women in non-state armed groups (NSAGs). Liberal feminism at this  point steps into the ring with jineologî, as the law becomes co-opted by the state in order to deny  the present needs of women in the periphery, instead focusing on a “national security threat”  presented by their past, a jineologî-based analysis would have placed more emphasis on Begum’s  own testimony. The needs of empire, capital, and struggle immediately conflicted in the case of women recruited to the Islamic State and resulted in a state ignoring the patriarchal and class power  intersections that led to a 15-year-old girl being recruited for ISIS, a more transnational, intersectional feminist analysis would have rejected the Islamophobic security narrative behind the UK’s decision.  

Alternative insurgent frameworks like Kurdistan's ideology of Jineolojî advocate for  women's liberation as central to revolution against the oppressive state and capitalist framework,  focusing on women’s participation in militarism, centering their agency, while also observing how  women have been excluded from knowledge production. This structure confronts securofeminism’s rigid understanding of women’s role, a direct confrontation of Global North  security and dominance logics from a Global South decolonial movement, which  understands patriarchy and state domination as essential to the other.

Jineolojî challenges positivist thinking, but in some interpretations fails to reject  bioessentialist thinking that has long been used against women. Statements like "Jineolojî camps  and workshops challenge the privileging of rationality and scientific thinking and to establish the importance of emotional intelligence and women's instinctive bond to the natural world" and "The main goal of Jineolojî is to uncover 'women's truths' in history, mythology, philosophy, and religion — women's lost essence" (Al-Ali and Käser, 2021b, p. 21) actually undermine women's material reality. Women's daily lives and lived experiences are crucial for knowledge production and are just,  if not more, valuable than what is produced in academia, but women's knowledge being delegated  as "herbalistic" and otherwise to the natural world is a miscomprehension of female reality. Women  are more than emotional, intuitive, "feeling" beings; they are just as analytical and rational as men, and their exclusion from academia and their relegation to this role is a result of misogyny. Women  are not ontologically connected with nature. Recall Simone de Beauvoir: “One is not born, but  rather becomes, a woman.” Belief in a female essence is reductive and regressive, stemming from the same logic that securofeminism posits, that the value and core agency of women comes from their occupation of nurturing and motherly roles.

Ocalan proposes that women free themselves from the inner "man" who behaves as a  “police officer” inside of all women. He advocates for the inclusion of women's knowledge into the  liberation movement, and notes that the construction of male identity has happened under  capitalist frameworks and narrative of nation and statehood. In quoting Ocalan: "Without  understanding how masculinity was socially formed, one cannot analyze the institution of state and  therefore will not be able to accurately define the war and power culture related to statehood"  (Al-Ali and Käser, 2021a, p. 19). Understanding his words in the context of Western colonialism  is essential. In the article Change Among the Gatekeepers, Connell writes that "Masculinities of the  colonizers in South Africa and T. Dunbar Moodie's (1994) study of the colonized, shows how a  gendered culture is created and transformed in relation to the international economy and  the political system of empire" (Connell, 2005 p. 1805).

Men’s role in all of this must be considered. Men cannot be incentivized out of  patriarchy under capitalism and its rewards; Ocalan is correct in saying that capitalism and  patriarchy rely upon the other, and women's participation in social revolution and rebuilding is  the only way to ensure equality. Even men who identify as feminists still benefit from the  standard operations of patriarchy such as the direct exploitation of the women in their lives for  personal gain: for example, the fact that their clothes are produced in sweatshops staffed by mostly  women, that the majority of people living in poverty globally are women, that there is a global  network of human trafficking exploiting the bodies of mainly women. What is the escape from  this? It is surely folly on women's part (and an intentional construction of the State's) to side  with securofeminist logics. It is also a folly to bury female identity in regards to the  liberation struggle, because socialism without feminism lurches towards liberalism. What will  the socialist men valiantly fighting against the state say about women when there is freedom?  Does their idea of liberation include women? Does it include giving up the luxuries afforded to them by women's unpaid labour, domestic and  otherwise? Do men have what it takes to sacrifice the “maleness” of their identity, given to them  and ruthlessly imposed by capitalist patriarchy?  

Perhaps it is not only insurgents who have been radicalized against the state, or future  terrorists by religion. As Abu-Lughod points out, words like extremism and radicalization are  empty signifiers that propose simple solutions, decontextualized from their historical and political  realities. However, perhaps feminists must ask if it is men who have been radicalized against  women. Male politicians bombing women and girls is war against women. Rape committed by  soldiers is a form of terrorism, as is the rape caried out by women’s male peers on a systematic level.  The social devaluing of women is a constant psychological attack, sometimes a quiet buzz, yet  always present, like drones. Perhaps what is needed is feminist insurgency against masculinism as a  whole (a theory lauded by feminists like Andrea Dworkin), as a response to patriarchal capitalism  and the warfare, both high and low intensity, that men deploy against women every day.
